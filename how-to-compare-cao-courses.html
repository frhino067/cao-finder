How to Compare CAO Courses: Look Beyond the Points

Similar names. Different experiences. A practical research guide to turn three possibilities into a shortlist you understand.

Start with three courses, not thirty

You search for business, engineering or computing and suddenly there are dozens of courses that sound almost identical. One has higher points. Another has a more impressive title. A third is closer to home. Which difference actually matters?

The useful comparison starts with what you would study and do there. This guide gives you a practical way to research three courses, record the differences and decide what to investigate next. You can use it alongside CAO Finder without needing to settle your entire career plan in one evening.

Choose a subject you are interested in and search it across a few colleges. Use location or points filters if they help narrow your research, then save three options that you would seriously consider. Three is a manageable starting point, not a recommended number of choices for your final CAO application.

Keep the exact course title, college, campus and CAO code beside every note. A general department page can describe opportunities that do not apply to the specific programme you are considering. The code helps you stay with the right course when you switch between CAO Finder, a prospectus and the college website.

Read the first year before the career list

Start with the compulsory first-year modules on the official college course page. If detailed descriptions are available, open them too. A course title tells you the area; the modules show the work you would encounter each week.

For each option, pick two modules you want to learn more about and one that concerns you. Do not reject a course because one topic looks difficult. Instead, ask whether you would be willing to practise that topic regularly. A challenge you are curious about feels different from a subject you have no interest in studying.

Translate module names into activities. Would you be reading and writing arguments, solving mathematical problems, coding, working in a laboratory, designing, presenting or analysing data? Write down what the published descriptions actually say. Where they are vague, mark the activity as a question rather than filling the gap yourself.

Look beyond first year as well. Identify when students choose options or a specialism. Check whether access depends on grades, available places or particular earlier modules. “There is a finance pathway” is less useful than understanding how a student reaches it and which choices remain open along the way.

Compare how you would show what you know

Two courses in the same subject can involve different assessment. Look for module handbooks or ask the department about the balance between examinations, coursework, presentations, practical work and group projects. Avoid assuming that a practical-sounding course has no exams.

Think about your own habits honestly. If you like projects but struggle to start them early, continuous assessment still requires planning. If you prefer exams, a course with frequent deadlines might help you spread your work, but it could also require a new routine.

Ask a specific question: “Can you describe a recent first-year assignment and how it was assessed?” This gives you something concrete to compare. Keep one example beside each course, and note whether it came from an official description, a lecturer or a current student. Those sources answer different questions.

Separate entry requirements from points

Check the college’s minimum entry rules and the specific course requirements before treating a points figure as your target. The CAO handbook explains that meeting the entry requirements comes first; enough points cannot replace a required subject or grade.

Make a separate checklist for subject requirements, grades and any additional assessment. If you have an exemption or a less common qualification, confirm how it applies with the institution. Use official information for your intended year of entry rather than assuming last year’s rules still apply.

CAO Finder’s points are a historical snapshot, not a prediction of your offer. Some entries involve additional assessment or a different scoring route. Read the notes on the course and follow the official links. If a requirement is not verified in the tool, that is a prompt to investigate, not evidence that there is no requirement.

Ask what placement and study abroad involve

Placement, internships and study abroad can be attractive, but the details matter. Find out whether an experience is part of the specific course, optional, compulsory or subject to a separate application. A department advertising an opportunity does not establish that every student will get it.

Ask when it happens, how students are selected, who arranges it and what support is available. If placement interests you, ask for examples of the work students have done. If study abroad is important, ask which partners are available to that programme and what academic conditions apply.

Record possible extra costs as questions to investigate, including travel or accommodation away from your usual base. Do not assume a placement is paid or that overseas study costs the same as staying at home. The most useful comparison distinguishes what is confirmed from what depends on circumstances.

Picture an ordinary Tuesday

A course has to fit into a real week. Check the campus where it is taught, then estimate the commute at the times you would actually travel. An open-day journey at midday may tell you little about arriving for an early class.

Compare the practical demands: class time, independent study, gaps between lectures and the journey home. Ask for a sample timetable if one is available. It is an example rather than a promise, but it helps you picture where study, training, work and rest could fit.

If moving away is possible, research accommodation options and the college’s current guidance. Create your own budget categories instead of using a single headline estimate: housing, transport, food, course materials and trips home. Use current figures from the relevant providers and record any assumptions.

You do not need a perfect forecast today. You do need to notice a practical obstacle early enough to investigate it. A course that looks attractive academically may still need a workable travel or accommodation plan before you can confidently choose it.

Put the differences on one page

Create three columns in a note or spreadsheet, one for each course. Use the same questions for all three. A blank cell is useful: it shows exactly what information you are missing.

  • Identity: exact title, course code, campus and qualification level.
  • Work: two interesting modules and one concern.
  • Assessment: a confirmed example and anything still unclear.
  • Entry: subject rules, grades and additional requirements.
  • Experience: placement, study abroad and their conditions.
  • Everyday life: commute or accommodation questions.
  • Next step: one question and the person or page that could answer it.

Do not immediately turn everything into a score out of ten. Numbers can make a guess look more certain than it is. First write the evidence and its source. If you later use scores, explain what each score means to you and keep unknown information visibly unknown.

Before asking someone else for advice, highlight the two differences that matter most to you. You might discover that you are comparing a subject you love with a commute you dislike, or a broad first year with a pathway that feels clearer. Naming that trade-off gives the conversation a purpose. A parent, teacher or student can then help you investigate the concern rather than simply naming their favourite college.

Keep personal priorities separate from eligibility. You might prefer a shorter commute or more project work, but you cannot trade those preferences against a compulsory subject requirement. Check whether an option is feasible, then compare how well it fits what you want.

A fictional comparison: same subject, different fit

Imagine a student called Alex comparing three fictional business courses. These are invented examples, not claims about named colleges. Alex initially prefers Course A because its previous points are highest.

After researching modules, Alex finds that Course A involves several compulsory subjects they are reluctant to study. Course B includes an area they enjoy, but its placement arrangements are unclear. Course C is nearby and has assessment examples that appeal to them.

Alex does not need to declare a winner immediately. The next useful step is to ask Course B’s department whether placement is compulsory and how it is arranged. They also read more about the work in Course A before deciding whether their concern reflects unfamiliarity or a real lack of interest.

The lesson is to make the question smaller. “Which college is best?” becomes “Which of these programmes gives me work I want to do, meets my entry situation and fits a realistic week?” That question is much easier to investigate.

Use CAO Finder for discovery, then verify the detail

Return to CAO Finder when research reveals a new interest. Try a search such as “Dublin business low points” or “engineering between 300 and 500 points”. You can also use the college, subject and location filters to explore another set of options.

Use the heart button to save a course in your browser. Your shortlist stays in that browser where storage is available; it does not sync across devices. Keep your comparison notes separately, particularly if you share a computer or regularly clear browsing data.

Open each course’s details and use a verified official course link where one is available. If the tool directs you to the college directory instead, search the exact code there. Record the official page you checked and the date, so you can revisit any information that matters before applying.

Finish with a next step, then build your preference

End your comparison by writing one sentence for each option: “I am keeping this because…” or “I need to find out…” Bring those sentences to your guidance counsellor, a course talk or an open day. A focused question is easier to answer than a request to choose your future for you.

When you arrange your CAO application, the official guidance is to rank courses by genuine preference rather than predicted points. Research helps you understand that preference. Read our order-of-preference guide for an explanation of how your ranking affects offers.

Set a small research task for your next session: check one module description, confirm one requirement or ask one placement question. Give yourself a stopping point instead of reopening every course page. You can revisit your priorities as you learn more, while keeping the notes that explain why an option stayed on your list.

You can be uncertain about a career and still make progress on a course decision. Start with a small set of options, examine the actual work and resolve one missing detail. A shortlist you can explain is a stronger starting point than a list built around reputation alone.
