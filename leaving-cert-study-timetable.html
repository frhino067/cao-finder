A Leaving Cert study timetable you can actually stick to

The best timetable is one you can use on an ordinary Wednesday. Build around the week you really have, then give each study block a clear job.

THE TAKEAWAY

Plan specific tasks in realistic spaces. Leave room for homework, breaks and the sessions that inevitably need to move.

Start with the fixed parts of your week

Put school, travel, meals, activities, family commitments and your usual bedtime into a weekly grid before adding revision. Homework already takes time: count it instead of pretending every evening begins with a blank page.

Now identify a few usable gaps. A short session after a busy day might be enough for one marked question. A quieter weekend morning might suit a longer piece of writing. Do not copy a classmate’s hours if their week is nothing like yours.

Replace subject labels with finish lines

“Maths, 7–8” is vague. “Attempt three differentiation questions, mark them and redo one error” tells you what completion looks like. Use the same approach across subjects: plan a comparative paragraph, practise one oral topic, or explain a diagram from memory before checking it.

Keep a small next-task list for each subject. If a teacher gives urgent homework, you can swap a block without spending twenty minutes deciding what to do. A timetable should reduce decisions, not create another assignment.

A sample week to adapt

Day Example revision task after homework
Monday Maths: three questions and an error review
Tuesday English: plan and write one timed paragraph
Wednesday Irish: practise one oral topic; check missing phrases
Thursday Economics: explain a concept and answer one application question
Friday Short catch-up or a planned evening off
Saturday Geography and another subject: one focused task each
Sunday Review the week and choose next week’s tasks

This is a structure, not a recommended number of hours. Swap subjects and session lengths to fit your own workload. Add breaks between demanding tasks rather than stretching one session until you stop concentrating.

Make room for weak topics without abandoning everything else

Choose one topic that is currently blocking progress and give it a clear next step. For maths, that might be revisiting an algebra skill before trying a harder question. For an essay subject, it might be getting feedback on how you develop a point.

Keep smaller maintenance tasks for stronger subjects. A whole week spent fixing one weakness can leave you behind elsewhere. If your list is impossible to fit, ask a teacher which tasks matter most before adding more hours.

Use a ten-minute weekly reset

Ask: what did I actually finish, what mistake keeps recurring, and what should I change next week? Move unfinished tasks deliberately. Do not simply copy an overloaded plan forward.

Protect time away from schoolwork. If the plan repeatedly runs into bedtime or leaves no recovery time, reduce the load and seek help prioritising. A sustainable timetable should serve you through the year, not look impressive for two days.

Use the attempt–check–repair cycle

A study block should produce evidence. Start by attempting a question, paragraph or recall task without looking at the answer. Check it against class notes, a marking scheme or teacher feedback. Then repair the weakest part and try a similar task again later. Simply rereading can feel smooth while hiding what you cannot yet produce on your own.

Keep a short error log with four headings: topic, what went wrong, the repair and the date to retry it. “Algebra—lost a minus sign—redo two examples—Thursday” is useful. “Bad at maths” is not. For essay subjects, record a precise issue such as weak evidence, an unclear topic sentence or failure to answer the wording of the question.

A rescue plan for weeks that fall apart

  1. Keep urgent homework and one priority revision task.
  2. Move—not duplicate—anything else into the next available space.
  3. Choose one 20-minute restart task that is easy to begin.
  4. At the weekend, remove tasks that no longer matter before planning again.

Missing a session is normal; trying to “pay it back” by doubling every later session usually creates a second failed plan. Judge the timetable by what it helps you complete across a month, not whether every box was ticked in one perfect week.

Link the work to a purpose. If your shortlist includes courses near a certain points range, calculate your current estimate using CAO Finder’s points calculator, then focus on the subject improvements that are both realistic and useful. Points should guide priorities, not turn every class test into a prediction of your future.

ONE MORE THING

How many hours should I study each night?

There is no single number that fits every student. Start with the tasks you need, account for homework and commitments, and adjust using what you actually manage to finish.
