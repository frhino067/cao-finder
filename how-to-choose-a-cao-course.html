Choosing a course

How to choose a CAO course when you have no idea what to study

You do not need a perfect career plan to choose a useful next step. Start with the work you enjoy doing, then test a small shortlist against real course details.

THE TAKEAWAY

Choose a course you can explain in terms of its modules and daily work, not just its title or the points attached to it.

Start with tasks, not job titles

“I want a good career” does not narrow down a course search very much. “I like arguing a case, interpreting graphs and explaining an idea” gives you something to work with. Write down three school tasks you would choose and three you consistently avoid. Include projects, practical work, presentations and independent reading.

Separate interest from marks. You may enjoy a subject you currently find difficult, or get good results in one you do not want to study for several years. Both observations matter. Ask what you would willingly spend more time learning if there were no test tomorrow.

Build a shortlist in three passes

  1. Explore: pick three subject areas and save a few courses from each. Keep this first list deliberately broad.
  2. Inspect: open the official course pages and read the compulsory first-year modules. Highlight what attracts you and what worries you.
  3. Test: compare teaching, assessment, location and practical constraints. Reduce the list only when you can give a reason.

Set a 30-minute limit for the first search. You are making a starting list, not a final life decision. A shorter research session with notes is more useful than dozens of open tabs you cannot remember.

Use a comparison you can actually defend

Question Evidence to collect
Will I like the work? Three compulsory modules and a sample assignment
How will I be assessed? Exams, projects, labs, essays or presentations
Can I manage the week? Campus, travel route and likely on-site commitments
What can come next? Placement details and named progression options

For example, two courses called Business might offer very different combinations of accounting, languages and placement. Do not give them the same score simply because the names match.

Check the deal-breakers before ranking

Confirm subject and grade requirements on each college’s current page. Entry requirements are separate from a points total; the CAO handbook explains why eligibility must be checked first.

Then decide your own non-negotiables: a manageable commute, a particular subject, or a placement you can realistically attend. Treat “I do not know yet” as a research task rather than silently giving a course a pass.

Finish with one useful conversation

Bring three course pages and your notes to a guidance counsellor, teacher or open day. Ask a specific question: “I like the policy modules but struggle with maths; what quantitative work is compulsory?” A precise question gives someone a chance to help.

Your next action is simple: choose three courses, write one reason for each, and identify one unanswered question. You can refine the shortlist as your knowledge improves.

Run a seven-day reality check

Before you move a course to the top of your list, spend one week noticing the type of work that gives you energy. Do not record only subject names. Record the activity: solving a problem, debating an issue, designing something, helping a person, analysing figures or writing an argument. A course title can be vague; repeated activities tell you much more about fit.

Next, sample the course rather than relying on its description. Read two first-year module outlines, watch an official lecture or open-day recording if one exists, and look at a typical assessment. Ask yourself whether you are interested in the questions the course asks—not whether every module sounds exciting. Almost every degree contains at least one compulsory topic you would not choose on its own.

Give each shortlisted course a score from one to five for interest, module fit, assessment fit, location and practical affordability. The total is not meant to make the decision for you. Its job is to expose the real disagreement. If one course wins on interest but loses badly on travel, you now know exactly what to investigate.

Use points as a filter, not a verdict

Previous CAO points show the score of the last applicant offered a place in that round; they are not a fixed entry price and can change when demand or available places change. Build a mixed shortlist instead of trying to predict one exact outcome. Include courses across a sensible range, but keep every course in your genuine order of preference.

Once you have a points estimate, use CAO Finder’s points calculator and course filters to discover realistic alternatives in the same subject area. Then open the college’s current page to verify requirements. The useful outcome is not a list of courses you are certain to get—it is a list where you understand why each option belongs.

ONE MORE THING

What if none of the courses feels perfect?

Look for a course with several things you want to learn and constraints you can manage. Explore further education and other routes with your guidance counsellor too; uncertainty is a reason to investigate, not to pick at random.
